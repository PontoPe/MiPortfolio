Mosaico

Designing a writing method that beginners could actually finish

Can a design method teach people to write stories? I designed and validated Mosaico, a step-by-step writing method adapted from design methodologies. In a two-group study, all 4 participants with zero writing experience completed full stories — and a teacher spontaneously asked to adopt the method in her own classroom.

The problem

A project with
no process

Writing a story is a project — but unlike design projects, it comes with no process. Beginners face a blank page with no steps, no checkpoints, no way to know if they're "doing it right." Most give up before finishing anything.

My hypothesis: design methodologies solve exactly this problem — they turn ambiguous creative work into navigable steps. Could they be adapted for narrative writing?

Can the scaffolding that helps designers ship products help writers finish stories?

The foundation

Nine methods,
one shared skeleton

I analyzed 9 design methodologies against 4 criteria: documented, user-centered, iterative, and outcome-driven. Then I mapped their converging goals to extract the shared skeleton — the steps that appear in every method regardless of author.

The four criteria

  • Documented — published and citable, not folklore.
  • User-centered — someone other than the author is in the loop.
  • Iterative — the method expects to be run more than once.
  • Outcome-driven — it ends in a thing, not in a feeling.
Sourcing decision For the narrative layer, I chose the six emotional arcs derived computationally from 1,737 books (University of Vermont, 2016) over purely literary taxonomies — I wanted structure backed by data, not tradition.

The tool

Three phases, borrowed
from the arc itself

Mosaico guides a writer through three phases taken from narrative arc structure itself. Each step suggests an adapted design tool: personas for imagining readers, empathy maps for characters, storyboards for timelines.

"The persona step reframes who will read this? — a question beginners never ask."

The adapted tools do the work the writing advice usually leaves implicit. A persona turns "write for your reader" into a filled-in sheet. An empathy map turns "make the character believable" into six questions with answers. The method never asks for talent it can't scaffold.

The guide

What using it
actually looks like

Mosaico ships as a guide, not a diagram. Sixteen steps across the three phases carry the spine of the process; ten auxiliary tools sit beside them as suggestions, each one lifted from a specific stage of the methodologies analysed and documented with its source.

3 phases · 16 steps · 10 auxiliary tools

The guide's own escape clause "The steps listed work as a guide to make writing easier. For each particular case it is possible to adjust or simplify the process according to the specific needs of your project."

That sentence is why 2 of 8 participants reordering the steps counted as a success and not a failure — the tool was written to be bent. It is also the difference between the spine and the suggestions: the four steps of Action end in internal validation, while focus groups stay in the optional column. A solo writer can run the whole method alone.

Worked example

The guide runs itself, once, in full

Rather than explain the method abstractly, the guide walks one story through all sixteen steps — a horror narrative on the Oedipus arc. The same brief later became the controlled prompt every participant in the study started from.

01

Exposition

  1. Goal: to entertain.
  2. Horror, on the Oedipus arc.
  3. Desk research on the reference horror titles.
  4. Synthesis of the closest ones — Evil Dead, Battle Royale, Asylum.
  5. Proto-persona: young adult, 23, drawn from people who watch horror on video platforms.
  6. Plot and time: young people finding supernatural powers through social media.

02

Action

  1. Focus group of 18–25-year-olds who consume horror — film and short-form video.
  2. Finding: they expect a male teenage lead, everyday digital slang, a family-curse thread, and exactly one survivor.
  3. Requirement — break the expectation: a female lead, a child, genuinely afraid of what she finds. No predictable ending, no happy ending, everything goes wrong.
  4. Character and setting: a small girl on her phone, at school.
  5. Validation: readers were surprised but interested — and suggested moving the story out of the school and into a house.

03

Resolution

  1. Two argument alternatives: she finds a strange message on her sister's forgotten phone, or she gets a strange DM while home alone.
  2. Two storyboards, one per alternative, marking the key beats.
  3. Readers preferred the first.
  4. Writing.
  5. Evaluation: people struggled to relate to the protagonist's choices.
  6. The character was adjusted.
  7. Validation beat expectations — nobody saw the ending coming.
  8. Revision, and the narrative closed.

"Step 5 of Action is the whole argument of this project: a reader told her to move the story, and the story moved."

Study design

Does the method reduce
the experience gap?

I designed the study around that single question — which meant the study had to be able to answer no. Two contrast groups, one controlled prompt, and data collected where I couldn't interfere with it.

Group A

4

Gen-Z, no writing experience

The people the method is for. If scaffolding works, it shows here first.

Group B

4

Educators experienced in professional writing

The contrast group. Their friction with the method is a different signal from a beginner's.

Controlled promptEveryone started from the same brief — horror genre, Oedipus arc — to isolate variation to the method, not the assignment.
One week, asyncIndividual diary studies over one week, minimizing researcher interference.
Then interviewsSemi-structured interviews after the writing was done, not during it.
Defining success first To make results measurable, I operationalized each dimension using Goals-Signals-Metrics (Rodden et al., Google) — defining what success would look like before running the study, not after.

Results

What the data showed

4/4

All four first-time writers completed full stories — and reported more enthusiasm at the end than the experienced group.

7/8

Recalled the method's steps by name after one week.

5/8

Needed to read the guide only once to use it.

2/8

Reordered the steps to fit their own process — which I counted as a success: the tool was designed to flex.

4/8

The moment that changed the project

During the evaluation interviews, something happened that I didn't design for:

One of the teachers in the study asked permission to use Mosaico with her own students.

Nobody recruited her. Nobody asked. The method was useful enough that a professional educator pulled it into her classroom on her own. In product terms: unsolicited adoption — the rarest signal an early-stage product can get. At the time, I noted it in a subsection of my thesis. It took me two years to understand it was the most important result in the study.

The hardest call

Removing the users from
a user-centered tool

6 of 8 participants questioned the external validation steps — recruiting focus groups of potential readers mid-process. The friction was real: a solo writer will never assemble a focus group, and steps that don't get used aren't steps, they're decoration.

Removed

External validation — recruit a focus group of potential readers and test the story mid-process.

east

Replaced with

Internal checkpoints — the writer reviews their own earlier decisions against the goal and audience they defined in phase one.

I still think about this trade-off. Listening to users meant removing the users. The tension never fully resolved — until the next version of the tool put real readers back in the loop by design (see: Mosaico v2).

Applied iteration

Three changes
that shipped

Change 01

A friendlier way in

Beginners reported the tool "looked too complicated" before even starting — a first-impression problem, not a usability one.

Change 02

Warmer visual language

Colour and type were doing the opposite of the method's promise — signalling expertise required.

Change 03

The validation restructure

The decision above, applied to the artefact — external validation out, internal checkpoints in.

What came out

Six stories.
One printed fanzine.

The study didn't produce opinions about writing. It produced writing: six complete horror stories, five published anonymously in a printed fanzine. The output of a method study should be the thing the method makes.

Evidence

The fanzine and the diaries

Limitations

What this study
couldn't claim

  • Samplen=8 is a qualitative signal, not statistical validation.
  • What was measuredI measured completion and satisfaction — not whether the stories were better than they would have been without the tool. No control group, no quality rubric.
  • InstrumentThe diary format produced thinner data than I hoped; several participants wrote minimal entries.

Every one of these gaps became a design requirement for what came next.

What comes next

Mosaico didn't
end in 2023

In 2026, the problem it solved — scaffolding thinking instead of replacing it — became the central problem in education: students now have AI that writes for them.

I'm rebuilding Mosaico as an AI writing companion that refuses to write, piloting with a real classroom, measured against Brazil's national essay rubric (ENEM).
